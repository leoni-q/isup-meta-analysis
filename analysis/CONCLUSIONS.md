# Interpretive summary and conclusions

**Evolution of ISUP Grade Group 1 Detection Across Prostate Cancer Screening Strategies**

This document interprets the completed meta-analysis in `REPORT.md`. It is a
summary of what the reported estimates support, what they do not establish, and
how the main limitations affect interpretation. It is not a clinical guideline
and does not replace assessment of mortality, quality of life, harms, or risk of
bias.

---

## Executive conclusion

The analysis supports a distinction between **inviting more men to screening**
and **changing the diagnostic pathway after screening has begun**.

1. **Screening invitation did not establish increased low-grade cancer
   detection, although every invitation trial pointed that way.** ERSPC, PLCO
   and CAP each found more GG1 detection per randomized or invited participant
   with invitation (trial RRs 1.27–2.25). The pooled RR was 1.573 (95% CI
   0.726–3.408), with extreme heterogeneity (I² 99.3%) and a prediction interval
   that included both a reduction and a large increase (0.335–7.378).

2. **Screening invitation did not establish increased GG≥2 detection.** The
   pooled RR was 0.993 (95% CI 0.886–1.113), with I² 64.6% and a prediction
   interval of 0.805–1.225. The evidence remains compatible with lower,
   unchanged, or higher GG≥2 detection.

3. **The strongest and most consistent result concerns diagnostic selectivity.**
   Among trials in which both arms were screened, the evaluated diagnostic or
   biopsy strategy increased the proportion of detected cancers that were
   GG≥2 (RR 1.388, 95% CI 1.233–1.563; I² 0%; prediction interval
   1.179–1.634). This result remained above the null after omission of any one
   trial.

4. **Improved GG≥2 yield among biopsied participants was not established.**
   With STHLM3-MRI excluded, the pooled RR was 1.875 (95% CI 0.861–4.085), with
   I² 25.3% and a prediction interval of 0.646–5.442. The point estimate favors
   newer pathways, but the uncertainty includes no effect.

5. **These findings do not show that any strategy improves survival or net
   clinical benefit.** The analysis concerns detection and diagnostic yield.
   Mortality, disease progression, quality of life, treatment burden, adverse
   events, and complete procedural harms were not analyzed.

The most defensible overall interpretation is therefore: **newer diagnostic
pathways appear better at concentrating GG≥2 disease among cancers found, while
their effect on GG≥2 yield among people biopsied remains uncertain; broad
screening invitation may increase GG1 detection, but neither that increase nor
any increase in GG≥2 detection was established.** No strategy can be declared clinically superior
from these outcomes alone.

## 1. What the randomized comparisons show

### Screening invitation versus no screening or usual care

| Outcome | Pooled result | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| GG1 per randomized/invited participant | RR 1.573 (0.726–3.408) | Every trial higher, but the pooled increase is not established; extreme inconsistency |
| GG1 among all detected cancers | RR 1.223 (0.952–1.572) | Point estimate favors a higher GG1 share; not established |
| GG≥2 per randomized/invited participant | RR 0.993 (0.886–1.113) | No clear increase or decrease established |
| GG≥2 among all detected cancers | RR 0.776 (0.469–1.286) | No clear change established |

The invitation-family results are dominated by between-trial differences:
I² ranged from 64.6% to 99.3%. All four prediction intervals crossed the null.
Consequently, the pooled values describe an average across substantially
different trials rather than a stable effect expected in every screening
program.

With three trials, the GG1 results are consistent in direction but not in size.
Each trial's own RR for GG1 detection (1.27–2.25) and for the GG1 share of all
cancers (1.09–1.32) is above 1, yet neither pooled confidence interval excludes
the null. The leave-one-out estimates, including the excluding-CAP and
excluding-ERSPC sensitivity analyses, rest on two trials each and are mostly
too imprecise to add information; for example, excluding CAP gives a GG1
detection RR of 1.687 (95% CI 0.044–64.583). The exception is the GG1 share
with PLCO omitted, RR 1.298 (95% CI 1.046–1.610). These analyses support
concern that invitation can increase low-grade detection, but they do not
establish it or provide a precise or broadly transportable estimate of its
size.

This pattern is compatible with additional detection of indolent disease, but
**overdiagnosis cannot be directly quantified from GG1 detection alone**.
Overdiagnosis requires evidence that a detected cancer would not have caused
symptoms or death during the person's lifetime.

### Diagnostic or biopsy strategy when both arms were screened

| Outcome | Pooled result | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| GG1 per randomized/invited participant | RR 0.563 (0.152–2.081) | Direction favors less GG1 detection, but highly uncertain |
| GG1 among all detected cancers | RR 0.567 (0.302–1.064) | Suggestive reduction, not conclusively established |
| GG≥2 per randomized/invited participant | RR 1.292 (0.571–2.925) | No clear population-level detection difference |
| GG≥2 among all detected cancers | RR 1.388 (1.233–1.563) | Consistent enrichment of GG≥2 among cancers detected |
| GG1 among participants biopsied | RR 1.002 (0.888–1.130) | STHLM3-MRI excluded; no clear difference |
| GG≥2 among participants biopsied | RR 1.875 (0.861–4.085) | STHLM3-MRI excluded; higher point estimate but no clear difference |

The distinction between denominators is essential. The evidence is strongest
for **who is selected for diagnosis and what grade is found**, not for an
increase in the absolute number of GG≥2 cancers detected across the randomized
population.

The GG≥2 share among all detected cancers is the most reliable comparative
finding in the report. It had no observed heterogeneity, its prediction interval
remained above 1, and every leave-one-out estimate remained above 1 with its
confidence interval excluding 1. The GG≥2 biopsy-yield point estimate is in the
same direction, but both its confidence and prediction intervals include 1
after excluding STHLM3-MRI.

Conversely, the apparent reductions in GG1 outcomes are not conclusive. Their
confidence and prediction intervals include no effect, and heterogeneity is
substantial for GG1 detection per randomized participant. The data therefore
support improved concentration of higher-grade disease more strongly than they
support a definite reduction in low-grade detection.

### Timing of screening initiation

PROBASE is a single timing contrast and should not be pooled conceptually with
screening invitation or diagnostic-strategy trials. Its very large GG≥2
detection RR (66.885) is driven by zero inferred GG≥2 events in the delayed
control arm and has an extremely wide confidence interval (4.098–1091.529).
The estimate changes markedly under alternative continuity corrections.

This result indicates that more GG≥2 cancers were found at the analyzed early
time point in the immediate-screening group. It does not provide a stable
estimate of long-term benefit and may partly reflect earlier diagnosis rather
than a lasting difference in cumulative disease detection.

## 2. Descriptive strategy patterns

The separate proportion models can generate hypotheses but cannot rank
strategies. They compare different studies and populations rather than
randomized head-to-head assignments.

Within that limitation, three patterns are notable:

- **Biomarker → MRI, now represented by STHLM3-MRI alone, showed the most
  selective profile:** GG1 comprised 15.0% of detected cancers, GG≥2 comprised
  85.0%, and 4.1% of screened participants underwent biopsy. No biopsy-yield
  estimate is available for this strategy, because STHLM3-MRI is excluded from
  the biopsy-yield analyses.
- **MRI-first pathways showed relatively high GG≥2 detection** per randomized
  or invited participant (4.4%) and a high GG≥2 biopsy yield (43.8%), but also
  the highest descriptive biopsy proportion (10.4%).
- **PSA-only estimates were highly inconsistent across studies.** I² was
  99.3%–100% for population detection, cancer-grade composition, and biopsy
  use, and remained high for biopsy yield. A single pooled PSA proportion is
  therefore a poor prediction for a specific program.

These values should not be interpreted as proof that biomarker → MRI is superior
to MRI-first, PSA → MRI, or PSA alone. Differences in trial populations,
eligibility, thresholds, follow-up, adherence, and outcome ascertainment can
produce the observed contrasts. Modern-strategy estimates contain at most two
study-strategy units, and Biomarker → MRI only one.

## 3. Robustness and evidential weight

### Findings with the strongest support

- Diagnostic/biopsy strategies increased GG≥2 among all detected cancers:
  RR 1.388 (95% CI 1.233–1.563), I² 0%, prediction interval 1.179–1.634.
- The direction and statistical conclusion were unchanged in every
  leave-one-out analysis.
### Findings that are suggestive but uncertain

- Diagnostic strategies may reduce GG1 as a share of detected cancers, but the
  95% CI narrowly includes no effect and the prediction interval is wide.
- Diagnostic/biopsy strategies had a higher GG≥2 biopsy-yield point estimate
  after excluding STHLM3-MRI (RR 1.875, 95% CI 0.861–4.085), but the confidence
  and prediction intervals include no effect.
- Screening invitation may increase GG1 detection: all three trials point that
  way, but the pooled confidence interval includes no effect and the size of the
  increase varies substantially by trial.

### Findings not established by this analysis

- A clear pooled effect of screening invitation on GG1 or GG≥2 detection.
- A clear effect of diagnostic strategy on GG≥2 detection across all randomized
  participants.
- A definitive reduction in GG1 detection from modern diagnostic pathways.
- Superiority of one named screening strategy over another.
- Reduction in prostate-cancer mortality, all-cause mortality, metastatic
  disease, treatment, or adverse outcomes.

## 4. Limitations that materially affect interpretation

1. **Few studies per model.** Comparative models contain at most four trials
   (three in the invitation family) and the largest descriptive model contains
   five. Estimates of heterogeneity,
   confidence intervals, and prediction intervals are consequently imprecise.

2. **Marked clinical and statistical heterogeneity.** Invitation trials differ
   in era, follow-up, adherence, background PSA use, screening intensity, and
   diagnostic work-up. The very high I² values are substantive, not merely
   statistical noise.

3. **CAP variance is not cluster-adjusted.** CAP randomized practices, but a
   suitable intracluster correlation or adjusted grade-specific variance was
   unavailable. Its confidence interval and weight may therefore be too
   precise.

4. **Control-group contamination and changing practice.** PLCO had substantial
   opportunistic PSA screening in the control arm. Older invitation trials and
   newer MRI-based trials represent different diagnostic eras.

5. **Missing and inconsistent denominators.** Invitation-family biopsy-yield
   RRs could not be estimated. CAP biopsy counts and grade counts refer to
   incompatible follow-up windows. STHLM3-MRI reports a GG≥2-specific biopsy
   denominator that differs from its general biopsy count and was excluded from
   the biopsy-yield analyses.

6. **Descriptive proportions are not causal comparisons.** Strategy-level
   proportions are confounded by study characteristics and generally rely on
   only two units for newer strategies.

7. **Outcome scope is incomplete for clinical decisions.** Publication bias
   could not be assessed; risk-of-bias judgments, PRISMA flow, treatment,
   procedural harms, adverse events, and mortality outcomes are absent.

8. **ProScreen is excluded.** ProScreen was extracted but is excluded from every
   analysis, which leaves three invitation trials and a single Biomarker → MRI
   trial.

---

## Final interpretation

The meta-analysis indicates that the principal measurable advantage of newer
diagnostic pathways is **greater diagnostic concentration of GG≥2 prostate
cancer**: a larger share of detected cancers and a larger share of biopsies
yield higher-grade disease. The evidence is less certain that these pathways
increase absolute GG≥2 detection across the randomized population or reliably
reduce GG1 detection.

By contrast, every invitation trial found more GG1 detection with invitation,
but the pooled increase is not established, and neither is any increase in GG≥2
detection.
Because effects varied greatly across invitation trials, the balance between
additional low-grade and higher-grade detection is likely to depend strongly on
the screening protocol, diagnostic pathway, adherence, background testing, and
follow-up period.

The appropriate clinical inference is cautious: **diagnostic refinement appears
more promising than screening expansion for improving the grade profile and
yield of detected disease**, but the report cannot determine whether that
improvement translates into fewer deaths, better quality of life, less
overtreatment, or a favorable overall benefit–harm balance. Those conclusions
require long-term clinical outcomes and a formal assessment of bias and evidence
certainty.
